## Context

The client manufactures using injection moulding. Materials and moulds move
through storage and a finishing area in high volume, and their location was
tracked informally — spreadsheets, labels, and the memory of the people who
work there. That works until it doesn't: as volume grew, finding a specific
material or mould, knowing what had happened to it, and holding movements
accountable all became slower and less reliable.

The engagement started narrow — manage storage and the finishing area — and
was deliberately kept that way until the core model proved itself. It then
grew, one validated step at a time, into an operational platform.

## Problem

I framed the problem in three layers, because each one pulls the design in a
different direction:

- **Operational.** Where is a given material or mould right now? What happened
  to it — every move, in order? Who moved it, and from where to where?
- **Structural.** The physical layout is not fixed. Rows are added, levels are
  re-sized, whole areas are re-purposed. The system has to absorb that without
  a code change or a developer in the loop.
- **Organisational.** Several roles touch the same data with different rights,
  and every change has to leave an auditable trail.

## Constraints

- **One engineer, working directly with the stakeholders.** Every decision had
  to be legible to me later and reversible if it turned out wrong. No room for
  architecture that needs a team to maintain.
- **It runs on a factory floor.** Mobile connectivity is intermittent, devices
  are shared, and the people using it are operators, not analysts. The UI has
  to be fast, forgiving, and hard to get wrong; the mobile client has to
  tolerate a bad network.
- **The floor changes faster than software releases.** Configuration has to win
  over code wherever a layout or a process might shift.
- **Auditability is not negotiable.** "What happened to this?" must always have
  a complete, ordered answer.
- **It has to stay cheap to run.** A small managed database and static hosting —
  no heavy infrastructure to justify.

## Approach

- **Model the warehouse as a tree the client owns:** area → row → grid → level →
  slot, with structure *types* so different physical layouts share one model.
  Operations staff re-shape the tree themselves.
- **Make every state change an event.** Current position and full history are
  both derived from an append-only movement log — there is one source of truth
  for "what happened".
- **Keep two read paths:** fast lookups for day-to-day operations, and
  paginated, filterable reporting with export for the office.
- **Give the layout a visual form** — 2D for speed, 3D for spatial understanding
  — both driven by the same structure data, not a separate drawing.
- **Deliver it where the work is:** web for administration and reporting, a
  mobile client for the floor, with synchronisation and explicit handling of
  conflicting edits.

## Architecture

<ArchitectureDiagram />

The clients are thin. The domain rules — what a valid structure looks like,
what a valid movement is, who may do what — live in one place, in the API's
service layer, and are covered by tests. Everything the floor and the office
see is the same data, kept in step by a realtime channel rather than by
people refreshing.

## Implementation

Work went in small, verifiable slices, each with tests that prove a behaviour
rather than chase a coverage number:

- **Domain rules in the service layer**, with contract tests around structure
  validation, movement integrity, concurrency, and authorization boundaries.
- **The optimistic-locking contract is verified by tests** that simulate two
  clients editing the same item, asserting that the second gets a conflict
  rather than a lost update.
- **Baseline hardening** from the start: login rate limiting, a locked-down
  CORS policy with correct preflight handling, and authorization checks on
  every write endpoint.
- **Every schema change is a migration.** The database shape is versioned and
  reproducible from zero.
- **The mobile client keeps local state** and reconciles on reconnect, so an
  operator can keep working through a network gap.

## Outcome

The operations team can locate materials and moulds, see exactly how stock has
moved and who moved it, and re-shape the storage structure themselves as the
floor changes — without waiting on a release. The platform is in active use
and continues to evolve with the operation.

## Future evolution

The following is being explored with the client. It is **not** part of the

- Acquisition of physical signals directly from the injection machines
  (ADAM / WISE devices)
- Production states, cycles and alarms derived from those signals
- Shop-floor dashboards
- Integration with production planning (PCP) and production orders

The current architecture — events as the source of truth, a realtime channel
already in place, a configurable structure model — was chosen partly so this
direction stays open without a rewrite. That is a design intent, not a promise.
